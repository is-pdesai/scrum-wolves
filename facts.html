Twelve facts to drop at parties

1 — They turned a river around.

When wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone in 1995, they reduced overgrazing by elk, which let willows and aspens regrow on riverbanks, which stabilized the soil, which changed the path of rivers. Ecologists call it a "trophic cascade." It is the most cited example of how one predator can reshape an entire landscape.

2 — Every dog is a wolf.

From a Chihuahua to a Great Dane, every domestic dog is taxonomically classified as Canis lupus familiaris — a subspecies of the gray wolf. Domestication began roughly 15,000–40,000 years ago.

3 — There is no such thing as an alpha wolf.

The "alpha" concept was based on a 1940s study of captive, unrelated wolves forced to live together. In a real wild pack, the "alpha pair" is just mom and dad. The biologist who popularized the term, L. David Mech, has spent decades trying to retract it.

4 — They can smell prey from a mile away.

A wolf has roughly 200 million olfactory receptors — about 100 times more than a human. They can scent a herd of elk over a mile downwind.

5 — Their howl is a contagious chord.

When one wolf howls, the rest of the pack joins in on intentionally different notes. The harmonic mismatch makes the pack sound larger than it actually is to any rival listening from a distance.

6 — They can run for hours.

A wolf can hit 38 mph in a sprint, but its real superpower is its cruising gait — about 5 mph, sustainable for hours. A pack tracking a wounded moose may cover 30 miles in a single night.

7 — Ravens follow them on purpose.

Ravens have learned that wolves mean leftovers. They sometimes lead wolves to carrion they can't open themselves. It's one of the best-documented working relationships between predator and bird.

8 — They mate for life.

Wolves form long-term pair bonds. Only the breeding pair in a pack reproduces; the rest of the pack — usually their grown offspring from previous years — helps raise the new pups.

9 — Pups are born almost helpless.

Wolf pups are born deaf, blind, and weighing about 1 lb. Within a year they're full-sized and hunting with the pack.

10 — They almost never attack humans.

In North America, fewer than two fatal wolf attacks on humans occur per decade. Bees, cows, and dogs each kill more Americans annually than wolves have in the past century combined.

11 — They were nearly wiped out.

By 1960, government bounty programs had eliminated wolves from 95% of their historical range in the lower 48 U.S. states. Recovery began with the Endangered Species Act in 1973 and accelerated with Yellowstone's reintroduction in 1995.

12 — They have a scent gland on their tail.

It's called the precaudal gland. They use it for individual identification and pack-marking. No, your dog has one too.
