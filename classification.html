08 · Scientific Name & Classification

Canis lupus.

The gray wolf was named by Carl Linnaeus in 1758. Two and a half centuries later, every dog you've ever met is still classified as one of its subspecies.

Subspecies

Taxonomists currently recognize roughly 30+ subspecies of Canis lupus, though the exact count is debated. The most commonly cited include:

  • Canis lupus lupus — Eurasian wolf. The "default" wolf across most of Europe and Asia.
  • Canis lupus occidentalis — Northwestern wolf. Largest subspecies; British Columbia, Alberta, Yukon, Alaska.
  • Canis lupus nubilus — Great Plains wolf. Central Canada and the upper Midwest US.
  • Canis lupus baileyi — Mexican wolf ("lobo"). Smallest North American subspecies; recovering in Arizona, New Mexico, and northern Mexico.
  • Canis lupus arctos — Arctic wolf. White-coated; Canadian high Arctic and Greenland.
  • Canis lupus pallipes — Indian wolf. South Asia.
  • Canis lupus dingo — Dingo. Australia. Sometimes treated as its own species (Canis dingo).
  • Canis lupus familiaris — The domestic dog. Yes, really.

Wait — dogs are wolves?

Technically: yes. The domestic dog was reclassified in 1993 from its own species (Canis familiaris) into a subspecies of Canis lupus, on the basis that dogs and wolves can interbreed and produce fertile offspring, and that genetic evidence places dogs squarely inside the wolf family tree. Dogs descended from a now-extinct Pleistocene wolf population somewhere between 15,000 and 40,000 years ago.

Some taxonomists push back and prefer to keep dogs as a separate species for practical reasons. The argument continues. Either way, your golden retriever and a Yukon timber wolf share more recent common ancestry than you and a chimp.
